BLOCK D. REASONING, AND HOW TO ACTUALLY SIT THE EXAM.

Play this one before session nine. It's shorter than the others. Half of it is the maths and puzzle section, and half of it is about how to behave in the room, which honestly might be worth more marks than anything else today.

First, the reasoning section.

Part of this paper isn't about programming at all. It's GRE style reasoning. Percentages, ratios, work rate, speed, number series and logic puzzles. These are fast marks if you recognise the shape, and a complete time sink if you try to reason from scratch. So here are the five shapes.

One. Percentage change. If something goes up twenty percent and then down twenty percent, it does not come back to where it started. A hundred goes up to a hundred and twenty, then down twenty percent of a hundred and twenty, which is twenty four, so you land on ninety six. You are four percent down. The rule is: always multiply, never add percentages. One point two times zero point eight is zero point nine six.

Two. Work rate. Convert everything to work per hour, then add. If A finishes a job in six hours, A does one sixth per hour. If B finishes in three hours, B does one third per hour, which is two sixths. Together that's three sixths, which is one half of the job per hour. So two hours. Never average the times. Averaging six and three to get four and a half is the wrong answer they're hoping for.

Three. Average speed. Also not an average. Sixty kilometres an hour going out, thirty coming back, is not forty five. Take sixty kilometres each way. One hour out, two hours back. A hundred and twenty kilometres in three hours. That's forty. The slower leg takes more time, so it weighs more.

Four. Ratios. Turn the ratio into parts. Split forty items three to five. Three plus five is eight parts. Forty divided by eight is five per part. So fifteen and twenty five. Always find the value of one part first.

Five. Number series. Write the differences underneath. Two, six, twelve, twenty, thirty. The gaps are four, six, eight, ten. So the next gap is twelve, and the answer is forty two. If the differences aren't obvious, take the differences of the differences.

Now the two eggs puzzle, because it's been asked at WellDev specifically.

Two eggs, a hundred floors. Find the highest floor an egg survives, using as few drops as possible in the worst case.

If you drop the first egg every ten floors, the worst case is nineteen drops. Ten jumps up, then nine steps back down.

The better answer is fourteen, and here's the idea. You use a shrinking step size. Start at floor fourteen. If it survives, go up thirteen more. Then twelve more. Then eleven. The reason is that every time the first egg survives, the linear search you'd have to do afterwards gets one floor shorter, so the step should get one shorter too. That balances the worst case so it's the same no matter where the egg breaks.

But listen. You are not expected to derive that under pressure. What you are expected to do is say the reasoning. I'd balance the two phases so the worst case is equal everywhere, which means a decreasing step size rather than a fixed one. Say that, and you've answered the question they were actually asking.

Now, the part that matters more than any of the maths.

How to sit this exam.

Rule one, and this is the big one. Ninety seconds, then guess and move on.

The way people fail a broad multiple choice paper is not by not knowing the material. It's by spending five minutes on one stubborn question and then running out of time on eight easy ones they could have answered instantly. One hard question is worth exactly the same as one easy question. Never trade four easy marks for one hard one.

So set the rule now, before you're in the room. If a question is still unresolved at ninety seconds, you pick the best looking option, you mark it, and you move on. If there's time at the end, you come back.

Which raises the one thing you should try to find out. Ask them whether there is negative marking. If there isn't, you should answer every single question, because a blank is worth nothing and a guess is worth something. If there is negative marking, then leave the ones you genuinely have no idea about. That one question changes your whole strategy, so ask it if you get the chance.

Rule two. Trace, don't guess.

Stack questions, tree traversals, sorting passes. These are all traceable on paper in about eight seconds. Write the letters down. Cross them off. Do not do it in your head under time pressure, because that's where careless mistakes come from, and a careless mistake on a question you actually know is the most expensive thing on the paper.

Rule three. Read the question word by word.

Is it asking for time complexity or space complexity? Best case or worst case? Which of these is stable, or which is not stable? The exam writers put the word not in there deliberately. Most of the marks people lose on questions they know are lost right there.

Rule four. Eliminate.

Even when you don't know, you usually know that two of the four options are wrong. Cross them out and you've turned a one in four guess into a coin flip. On a long paper that difference is real.

Rule five. Don't panic at an unfamiliar subject.

This paper covers nine subjects. You will not know all of them equally, and nobody does. A former WellDev interviewer said publicly that most of their questions are easy and any fresh graduate should have a basic idea, and that what they actually look for is people who can handle the unconventional ones. That means composure is the thing being tested. Hitting a question you've never seen is expected. It is not a sign that you're failing.

Now, the mock.

Your next session is forty questions in forty five minutes. That's about sixty five seconds each. Phone in another room. No notes, no searching. Treat it like the real thing, because the only reason to do it is to practise the pacing.

When you finish, score it, and then do the thing most people skip. Look at where the wrong answers clustered. Not how many you got wrong, but which subject they were in. That tells you exactly what to spend the repair session on. One weak subject fixed at seven in the evening is worth more than a general re read of everything.

And then stop. Twenty one hundred, you're done. A tired brain on exam morning costs you more than the last hour of cramming ever gains you. Sleep is part of the preparation, not the thing you sacrifice for it.

Three things to carry out of this block.

One. Ninety seconds, then guess and move on. One stubborn question costs four easy ones.

Two. Ask whether there's negative marking, because it decides whether you guess on everything or not.

Three. Trace on paper, read the question twice, and eliminate the obviously wrong options.

Go and do the timed reasoning questions now. With a clock.
